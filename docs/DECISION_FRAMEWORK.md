# Whole-student decision framework

An Opportunity can be academically open and still be unaffordable, impractical, unsupported, or simply wrong for the student. The product should help students reason across distinct dimensions without pretending there is one objectively best institution.

## 1. Academic access

Can the student submit an eligible application for this Intake?

- IB Diploma status, total points, subjects, levels, and grades;
- language evidence and qualification equivalencies;
- tests, portfolio, audition, interview, essay, reference, or activity requirements;
- application round, quota, deadline, and documentation rules;
- competitive selection factors shown separately from minimum requirements.

## 2. Financial feasibility

Can the student realistically fund the complete path, not just tuition?

- tuition and mandatory institutional or application fees;
- estimated housing, food, insurance, travel, residence permits, and deposits;
- citizenship- and residency-specific fee status;
- grants, scholarships, loans, work limits, and eligibility conditions;
- currency, price year, assumptions, and a range rather than false precision.

Show a transparent scenario such as “EU student living away from home” rather than a universal cost number. Never label a programme affordable without the student's own budget and assumptions.

## 3. Practical feasibility

Can the student complete the real-world steps in time?

- application and result timelines;
- visa, residence, insurance, and proof-of-funds steps;
- housing availability and when queues or applications open;
- required local language for study, placements, licensing, work, or daily administration;
- travel time and distance from home;
- document legalization, translations, credential services, or centralized portals.

## 4. Learning fit

How is the student likely to learn there?

- curriculum structure and room to change direction;
- teaching format, class scale, contact hours, and independent study;
- assessment style: examinations, coursework, projects, oral work, or practical assessment;
- placements, internships, study abroad, research, studios, laboratories, and industry projects;
- academic calendar, duration, credential, and progression rules.

Use official curriculum and programme sources where possible. Student stories can add texture but are not evidence of a universal experience.

## 5. Support and belonging

What support can a student inspect before disclosing anything personal?

- disability accommodations and the process and timing for requesting them;
- academic advising, tutoring, language, mental-health, and wellbeing services;
- international-student and immigration support;
- housing support and emergency assistance;
- clubs, communities, faith facilities, sport, and social infrastructure;
- campus and city accessibility.

Do not ask students to reveal sensitive identities merely to show available support. Provide direct official links and make clear when availability does not guarantee an individual accommodation.

## 6. Future options

What does the Programme qualify the student to do next?

- institutional and programme accreditation;
- professional recognition and jurisdiction-specific licensing limits;
- access to postgraduate study;
- graduate work routes and relevant language constraints;
- published graduate outcomes, with methodology and population clearly stated.

Avoid salary promises and unqualified employment claims. Professional recognition can vary by destination and by where the student later intends to work.

## 7. Personal preference

What makes the student curious, comfortable, or energized?

- field and subject interests;
- city, town, rural, campus, or distributed setting;
- climate, geography, language environment, and distance from home;
- institution scale and community style;
- willingness to learn a local language or travel far.

These are not lesser criteria. Keep them student-controlled and reversible rather than turning them into hidden recommendation weights.

## How comparisons should work

Present each dimension separately with a coverage and freshness indicator. A student may mark personal priorities, but the system should explain any ordering and allow sorting by a single transparent factor.

Useful comparison language includes:

- **Meets / does not meet / needs review** for published academic requirements;
- **Within / near / above your stated range** for a student-supplied budget scenario;
- **Known step / possible blocker / unresolved** for practical feasibility;
- **Matches your preference / differs from your preference** for student-controlled choices.

Avoid a single “fit score,” star rating, or red-amber-green summary that conceals missing evidence or subjective weighting.
